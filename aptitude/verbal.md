# 1. Verbal Strategy

Verbal aptitude rewards **precision under time pressure** more than complicated vocabulary.

For grammar questions:

```text
Identify subject → identify verb → inspect tense → inspect modifier → inspect parallel structure
```

For reading comprehension:

```text
Question → locate relevant passage → verify evidence → eliminate unsupported options
```

For vocabulary:

```text
Context → root/prefix/suffix → tone → eliminate impossible meanings
```

# 2. Parts of Speech

| Type | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Person/place/thing/idea | engineer |
| Pronoun | Replaces noun | they |
| Verb | Action/state | build |
| Adjective | Modifies noun | efficient |
| Adverb | Modifies verb/adjective/adverb | quickly |
| Preposition | Relationship | in, on, at |
| Conjunction | Connects | and, but, although |
| Interjection | Emotion | wow |

Correctly identifying the grammatical role often makes sentence correction much easier.

# 3. Subject-Verb Agreement

The verb agrees with the grammatical subject, not necessarily the nearest noun.

### Example

> The list of requirements **is** long.

Subject = “list,” not “requirements.”

## Common traps

- Each/every → usually singular verb.
- Neither/nor and either/or → verb often agrees with the nearer subject in standard constructions.
- Collective nouns depend on intended usage/context.
- “One of the engineers **is** ...”

### Quick test

Remove the interrupting phrase:

```text
The list [of requirements] is long.
```

# 4. Tenses

## Present Simple

Habits, facts, regular events.

> She works remotely.

## Present Continuous

Action happening around now.

> She is working remotely.

## Present Perfect

Past action connected to the present.

> She has completed the report.

## Past Simple

Completed past event.

> She completed the report yesterday.

## Past Perfect

An earlier past event relative to another past event.

> She had completed the report before the meeting.

## Future

Use the construction appropriate to intention, prediction, arrangement, or schedule.

**Trap:** do not mechanically choose a tense based only on a time word; inspect the relationship between events.

# 5. Articles

## A / An

Use with a singular countable noun when it is non-specific.

The choice depends on **sound**, not spelling:

- an hour
- a university

## The

Use when the noun is specific/identified in context.

## Zero article

Often used for general plural/uncountable concepts:

> Computers are useful.

> Information is valuable.

# 6. Prepositions

Common combinations should be learned as phrases rather than isolated words.

Examples:

- interested **in**
- responsible **for**
- capable **of**
- depend **on**
- comply **with**
- similar **to**
- different **from**
- good **at**

### Time

- at 5 PM
- on Monday
- in August
- in 2026

# 7. Pronouns

A pronoun should clearly refer to the intended antecedent and agree in number/person.

Bad:

> When the manager spoke to the developer, **he** was upset.

Who is “he”?

Good writing removes ambiguity.

### Who vs whom

A useful formal test:

```text
he → who
him → whom
```

Example:

> Who called?

> Whom did you call?

# 8. Modifiers & Parallelism

## Dangling Modifier

The opening phrase must logically modify the subject that follows.

Incorrect:

> Walking into the office, the laptop was on the desk.

The laptop did not walk.

Better:

> Walking into the office, I saw the laptop on the desk.

## Parallelism

Items in a list should use compatible grammatical forms.

Incorrect:

> The role requires coding, testing, and to document systems.

Better:

> The role requires coding, testing, and documenting systems.

---

# 9. Active & Passive Voice

Active:

> The engineer fixed the bug.

Passive:

> The bug was fixed by the engineer.

Use passive voice when the action/result matters more than the actor or when the actor is unknown.

For aptitude questions, preserve tense when converting voice.

# 10. Direct & Indirect Speech

Direct:

> She said, “I am busy.”

Indirect:

> She said that she was busy.

Check:

- Pronoun changes.
- Tense backshift where required.
- Time/place expressions.
- Question structure.

# 11. Vocabulary

## Learn words by context

Instead of memorizing isolated definitions, learn:

```text
word → meaning → synonym → antonym → example sentence → common collocation
```

## Prefixes

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| anti- | against | antifreeze |
| auto- | self | automatic |
| bi- | two | bilingual |
| inter- | between | international |
| micro- | small | microscope |
| pre- | before | preview |
| re- | again | rebuild |
| sub- | under | submarine |

## Common roots

- `bene` → good
- `mal` → bad
- `dict` → say/speak
- `spect` → look
- `scrib/script` → write
- `port` → carry
- `tract` → pull/draw

# 12. Synonyms & Antonyms

Do not select a word that is merely related. Match the **meaning and tone**.

Example:

> “concise” = brief and clear

“short” may be related, but “concise” specifically implies efficient expression.

For antonyms, check whether the relationship is:

- Exact opposite.
- Degree-based.
- Context-dependent.

# 13. Sentence Correction

Check in this order:

1. Subject-verb agreement.
2. Pronoun reference.
3. Tense.
4. Articles.
5. Preposition.
6. Modifier placement.
7. Parallelism.
8. Comparisons.
9. Idiomatic usage.
10. Meaning and clarity.

Avoid changing a sentence merely because another option “sounds nicer.”

---

# 14. Error Detection

Split the sentence into grammatical units.

Example:

```text
The manager / along with the engineers / are attending / the meeting.
```

The subject is “manager”; “along with the engineers” is an interrupting phrase.

Correct:

> The manager, along with the engineers, **is** attending the meeting.

# 15. Fill in the Blanks

### Strategy

1. Read the whole sentence.
2. Predict the meaning before seeing options.
3. Determine grammatical form.
4. Use context/tone.
5. Eliminate contradictions.
6. Check the completed sentence.

For two blanks, solve the relationship between the blanks before choosing words independently.

# 16. Para-Jumbles

Find the sentence that establishes the topic/context first.

### Strong clues

- “This/these/it/they” refers backward.
- “However/therefore/therefore” signals logical relation.
- A definition often precedes examples.
- General idea usually precedes supporting details.
- Chronological steps should preserve time order.

### Method

```text
Find opening sentence
→ find mandatory pairs
→ connect transitions/pronouns
→ place conclusion
→ verify full paragraph
```

Do not rely only on sentence length or vocabulary style.

# 17. Reading Comprehension

## Question types

- Main idea.
- Detail.
- Inference.
- Tone.
- Purpose.
- Vocabulary in context.
- Author's assumption.

## Main idea

Ask:

> What is the passage primarily trying to communicate?

## Inference

Choose what is strongly supported, not what is merely plausible.

## Tone

Watch for words showing:

- approval
- skepticism
- criticism
- neutrality
- optimism
- caution

### RC technique

For long passages:

```text
Read question → scan passage for anchor → read local context → verify answer
```

For short conceptual passages, reading the full passage first may be faster.

# 18. Critical Reasoning

Identify:

```text
Conclusion
   ↑
Evidence / premises
   ↑
Assumptions
```

## Strengthen

Adds evidence that makes the conclusion more likely.

## Weaken

Provides evidence that reduces confidence in the conclusion.

## Necessary assumption

Must be true for the argument to work.

### Negation test

Negate the candidate assumption. If the argument collapses, the assumption is likely necessary.

# 19. Sentence Completion

Use semantic and grammatical constraints together.

Look for:

- Contrast words: although, however, despite.
- Cause words: because, therefore, hence.
- Continuation: moreover, furthermore.
- Degree: barely, extremely, relatively.

The correct answer must fit both **meaning** and **grammar**.

# 20. Common Confusions

| Confusion | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| affect / effect | affect usually verb; effect usually noun, with exceptions |
| fewer / less | countable vs generally uncountable |
| its / it's | possessive / it is or it has |
| your / you're | possessive / you are |
| their / there / they're | possessive / place / they are |
| than / then | comparison / sequence |
| principal / principle | person or main / rule |
| stationary / stationery | not moving / writing materials |
| complement / compliment | completes / praise |
| advice / advise | noun / verb |
| accept / except | receive/agree / exclude |
| imply / infer | speaker suggests / listener concludes |

# 21. Interview Communication

Verbal ability also matters in interviews beyond multiple-choice questions.

## Answer structure

For behavioral questions:

```text
Situation → Task → Action → Result
```

## Technical explanation

Prefer:

```text
Problem → approach → trade-off → result
```

Avoid:

- Excessive filler words.
- Overly long answers.
- Memorized-sounding phrases.
- Unclear ownership of work.
- Unsupported claims.

Use precise language:

> “I reduced the query latency from X to Y by changing ...”

rather than:

> “I made it much faster.”
